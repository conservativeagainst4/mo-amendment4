John G Fraser
U.S. Representative — District 3
Response received: July 20, 2026
✓ Opposes Amendment 4

Hi Darrin,

Happy to share my thoughts. I am hoping all of this can be in your message to voters.

The U.S. Constitution requires 75% (38) of the states to ratify a change to the constitution. I could be in favor of something like this at the state level. However, this amendment is not similar. If it required the same percentage, that would be 6 of 8 districts.

I think the argument that the amendment would lessen dark money influence is exactly backwards; the dark money would only have to spend money in one district to get what they want.

For these reasons, I cannot support 4.

Best wishes,
John

Brett Hueffmeier
U.S. Representative — District 5
Response received: July 20, 2026
✗ Supports Amendment 4

Dear Mr. Wright,

I support Amendment 4 because passage of a constitutional amendment is intentionally meant to be a cumbersome process so that the democratic instruments of government are preserved and can endure over time. Amending the constitution is not intended to be a way of permanently legislating on issues of policy. Its purpose should be to effectuate changes in the process, procedures, and methods of carrying out the work of government and the delegation of powers between its branches.

I believe the current system of gathering petitions and suspending existing laws passed by the legislature and signed by the executive branch is inherently suspect because of the methods used to portray the purpose and necessity of the positions behind them in obtaining required petition signatures to advance an amendment. Like with the federal constitution, the process should be subject to republican methods of ratification, which is done through passage by most geographic territories. This helps ensure the process of ratification of constitutional amendments are not the result simply of popular passions and that it remains enduring in meaning and purpose over time.

That process should involve the participation of the legislature and the public if they are to be subject to a simple majority for ratification. Alternatively, if the public seeks to change the constitution on its own, the process of acting on such measures unilaterally should be checked and made more cumbersome by requiring that ratification is agreed to by the congressional districts as units.

This avoids constitutional changes based only on momentary impulses, something the constitutions of states or any unit of government are supposed to be insulated from. Otherwise, the faith in the constitution itself risks degradation over time and, with it, so does our system of democratic participation.

Regards,
Brett Hueffmeier

Brett Hueffmeier — Candidate for U.S. Congress, Missouri’s 5th Congressional District
